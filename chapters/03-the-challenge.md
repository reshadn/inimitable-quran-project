# The Challenge

*Research draft dated 20 September 2026. Translations are original working renderings from the Arabic unless otherwise stated. Commentary passages have been inspected at the linked locations; the complete works and their manuscript histories have not been reviewed. Independent specialist review remains pending.*

An accusation can explain very little. To say that a speaker invented something identifies a possible origin, but it does not yet explain the achievement. What capacities would the invention require? Which resources would make it possible? What should other capable people be able to accomplish under comparable conditions?

The Qur’an repeatedly brings the accusation of fabrication into contact with a demand for production. If this discourse is something a human being has composed, its opponents are challenged to bring something comparable. The move deserves examination because it asks criticism to become more specific. Instead of merely assigning the text to a familiar category, the critic must confront what that assignment is supposed to explain.

This does not make the challenge self-proving. An exceptional human composer might accomplish something that other people cannot reproduce. Nor does an invitation to compete automatically supply an agreed method of judging the result. But these objections identify work the argument must do. They do not remove the question the challenge opens: what accounts for the particular achievement before us?

## What the text asks

In 2:23, the address begins with doubt about what has been sent down to God’s servant. Its central imperative is:

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">
فَأْتُوا بِسُورَةٍ مِنْ مِثْلِهِ
</div>

An initial working rendering is: **“Then bring a surah like it.”** The verse continues by inviting the addressees to call their *shuhadāʾ* apart from God, if they are truthful. Whether that word emphasizes witnesses, supporters, or those able to attest a competing composition requires explanation. Even the short rendering of the imperative leaves an interpretive question: what does “it” refer to? [CH-01: 2:23–24](https://quran.com/al-baqarah/23-24)

Al-Ṭabarī makes that question explicit. He records an interpretation referring the pronoun to the Qur’an and another referring it to Muhammad as a human being. He prefers the former, using the parallel demand in 10:38 to support his reading. His discussion also emphasizes that the challenge addresses people through their own Arabic language. It does not demand competence in a language foreign to them. Later in the same commentary, he records explanations of *shuhadāʾ* involving assistance and testimony. These are inspected features of his interpretation, not proof that every commentator understood the verse identically. [CH-06: al-Ṭabarī, commentary on 2:23, discussion of “a surah from its like” and “your witnesses”](https://quran.com/al-baqarah/23/tafsirs/ar-tafsir-al-tabari)

The next verse matters as much as the invitation. In a close working rendering, 2:24 says, “But if you do not, and you will not, then fear the Fire.” The verse proceeds to describe that fire and those for whom it is prepared. The challenge is therefore situated within religious warning. The book should neither suppress that setting to make the passage sound like a neutral competition nor treat its warning as a substitute for evidence addressed to a doubtful reader. [CH-01](https://quran.com/al-baqarah/23-24)

Other passages vary the terms of the demand. In 10:37, the Qur’an rejects an origin in fabrication apart from God and presents itself in relation to preceding revelation and the exposition of scripture. Verse 38 then confronts the allegation that the Messenger fabricated it: “Say: Then bring a surah like it, and call whomever you can apart from God, if you are truthful.” The immediate subject is the origin of a discourse that makes religious and epistemic claims. Pleasant sound alone would be an inadequate account of what is at issue. [CH-02: 10:37–38](https://quran.com/yunus/37-38)

In 11:13 the demand is for ten surahs, described as *muftarayāt*, invented or fabricated. Al-Qurṭubī explains the imperative against the opponents’ allegation: if they say the Messenger invented the discourse, let them produce its like as something invented according to their claim. This makes the invitation more pointed. A respondent cannot simply say that genuine revelation is unavailable to a human competitor; the verse challenges the account in which the discourse is fabricated. Verse 14 then directs its audience, upon the failure of a response, toward acknowledgment of divine knowledge and submission. The passage itself makes the inference theological. Whether a contemporary reader should accept that inference remains something to investigate. [CH-03: 11:13–14](https://quran.com/hud/13-14), [CH-07: al-Qurṭubī on 11:13](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/qortobi/sura11-aya13.html)

The scale expands in 17:88. In our working rendering: “Say: If humans and jinn gathered to bring the like of this Qur’an, they would not bring its like, even if they supported one another.” This is a claim extending beyond a particular unsuccessful individual. It includes cooperation and reaches beyond human beings. A literary experiment with recruited readers cannot test everything asserted here. It can examine evidence relevant to part of the claim. [CH-04: 17:88](https://quran.com/al-isra/88)

Finally, 52:33–34 again places the accusation of invention beside a demand: “Then let them bring a discourse like it, if they are truthful.” The Arabic noun here is *ḥadīth*, rendered “discourse,” rather than a specified number of surahs. Al-Qurṭubī explains it in relation to producing something resembling the Qur’an from their own resources. This verse should not be paraphrased as explicitly demanding a single sentence, a fixed word count, or precisely one surah. Those specifications are absent from its wording. [CH-05: 52:33–34](https://quran.com/at-tur/33-34), [CH-08: al-Qurṭubī on 52:34](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/qortobi/sura52-aya34.html)

These passages can be compared without inventing a timeline. Their order in the muṣḥaf is not itself a chronology of proclamation. A historical account in which the challenge moves through successive stages requires historical evidence and an account of disagreement about dating. For this chapter, the secure observation is that the received text contains these different formulations. Ibn Kathīr’s commentary on 2:23 also gathers several of them into a connected discussion, showing a classical practice of reading them together. [CH-09: Ibn Kathīr on 2:23](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/katheer/sura2-aya23.html)

## The difficulty within “like it”

The repeated word *mithl* makes comparison central. Yet “like” is always like in some respect. A handwritten copy resembles its source, but copying would not demonstrate an independent capacity to compose it. A sentence containing the same number of words might match its length while failing in meaning. Conversely, an accomplished passage need not share the same rhyme to perform comparable literary work.

We should resist two temptations. One makes resemblance so easy that any imitation of surface features counts as success. The other makes it so restrictive that nothing except the original could qualify. Both empty the challenge of its intellectual interest. A contest in which mere rhyme suffices scarcely examines the achievement. A contest in which every difference disqualifies the respondent cannot distinguish inimitability from identity.

The project’s proposed reading is therefore concerned with **comparable achievement**: the relationship among expression, intelligible meaning, communicative purpose, and composition. It is a proposal for investigating the challenge, not a claim that these verses contain a modern assessment manual. Its adequacy must itself be discussed with Arabic specialists and readers whose commitments differ.

Consider what this means in practice. A short surah may combine a compact argument, a memorable sequence of sounds, and an address that changes the listener’s position. A fair comparison would ask whether another complete composition accomplishes comparably demanding work, with comparable economy and control. It would also allow the alternative to achieve something through a different organization. The comparison must identify shared demands without pretending that all good writing pursues the same purpose.

The word “complete” is essential. A single impressive phrase can teach us to notice grammar and imagery. The demand for a surah brings arrangement and development into the inquiry. How do its parts depend on one another? Does an ending complete what the opening starts? Are repetition and transition functional? What would a proposed revision improve, and what might it damage? Such questions give the comparison substance before a final verdict is attempted.

There is no reason to settle these questions by counting rhetorical devices. More metaphor is not necessarily better than less metaphor. A powerful warning may depend on directness. A prayer may gain force through repetition. The question concerns what the language accomplishes in its setting and how the parts of that accomplishment interact.

## Why the challenge is intellectually serious

The invitation to seek assistance makes the Qur’anic claim unusually demanding. Its explanation cannot rest on one opponent’s lack of talent. On the reading advanced here, the challenge directs attention to a possible disparity between resources available to opponents and the capacity to produce a comparable composition. That is a reason to investigate the strongest alternatives, not merely the most easily dismissed ones.

The affirmative argument begins with this explanatory pressure. If careful examination disclosed a sustained conjunction of achievements, and if strong human comparisons repeatedly illuminated rather than dissolved that conjunction, the evidence could support further inquiry into revelation. The relevant question would be why the whole profile occurs together, at the scale and within the circumstances established by the evidence. No one admired feature would have to carry the conclusion alone.

For example, economy may ordinarily impose a cost on explicitness. A composition might remain clear while opening several contextually defensible implications. Sound patterning may impose pressure on word selection. A passage might sustain a sound pattern while choosing an expression whose grammar is particularly apt. These are possible interactions worth examining. They are not results already established for every surah, and a striking interaction may still be humanly achievable. The positive research task is to discover which accounts survive sustained comparison.

The strongest human explanation deserves equal specificity. It might appeal to exceptional linguistic ability, long engagement with oral discourse, religious imagination, revision, interaction with hearers, or processes of transmission. Those possibilities should be investigated in relation to historical evidence. Merely listing them does not establish that they occurred or explain the details of the text. Equally, difficulty documenting them does not by itself establish divine authorship.

The argument improves when each explanation is asked to account for the same evidence. A proponent should not appeal to the Qur’an’s precision when convenient and excuse every difficulty as inaccessible mystery. A critic should not treat “human genius” as an explanation requiring no account of capacities, circumstances, or textual effects. Both positions can become insulated from evidence. Neither needs to be.

## What would make an investigation fair?

Before assessing a proposed competitor, we should specify the passage, its communicative task, the relevant comparison, and the reasons for selecting it. Those choices should be visible before judgments arrive. Otherwise an evaluator can shift the criterion whenever a rival succeeds: praise clarity until a clearer rival appears, then praise ambiguity; praise brevity until a shorter rival appears, then insist on fullness.

Readers should distinguish an observable feature from an interpretation and an evaluation. A repeated grammatical form can be located. An account of its contribution requires argument. A judgment that the result excels requires standards and comparison. Agreement at the first level does not force agreement at the last, but it gives disagreement something more precise to concern itself with.

Mixed panels of competent readers could help reveal where judgments converge and where they depend on prior commitments. Anonymizing a text would sometimes be useful, but recognizable Qur’anic language creates an obvious limitation. Recognition must be measured or reported, not wished away. Such studies are proposed work for this project. No panel has certified the argument developed here.

What could weaken it? Persuasive evidence that central interpretations depend on arbitrary divisions would matter. So would strong alternative compositions judged comparable under standards announced beforehand. Historical evidence that substantially improves a human account would also matter. The appropriate response would be to revise the claim at the affected level, rather than declare every adverse result irrelevant.

Conversely, an unsuccessful entrant establishes only that this entrant, under these conditions and these criteria, did not succeed. A hundred unsuccessful entrants would still not constitute a deductive proof about every possible human composition. The universal claim of 17:88 is stronger than any finite contest result. A cumulative argument may draw on such evidence, but it must explain its inferential steps.

The invitation before us is substantial enough without shortcuts. The Qur’an places a demanding claim about its origin beside a demand that opponents address its achievement. Our task is to make that achievement inspectable, to give strong alternatives room to succeed, and to explain why the resulting evidence does or does not support revelation. The next step is therefore neither applause nor dismissal. It is to read closely enough that “like it” becomes a question we can discuss with reasons.
